\begin{abstract}
KV-cache eviction is deployed as a uniform accuracy-memory trade, but its per-input effect is bimodal. On many long-context inputs a compressed cache
matches the full one, while on precise multi-key retrieval the same evictor
drives accuracy from $99\%$ to $0\%$. Existing evictors choose which tokens to
keep under a fixed budget and report benchmark means that average this failure
away, never treating whether to evict as a decision. We show that one label-free scalar read from prefill attention identifies the regime before any
decoding: the early-to-late drop $D$ in mean pairwise top-$k$ Jaccard
agreement between attention heads, which stays small when heads converge on
the few tokens carrying the answer. \method thresholds $D$, running any
SnapKV-style evictor when the drop is large and keeping the full cache when it
is small, with no training, labels, or fine-tuning. Across four base evictors,
four models, RULER, and LongBench, gating cuts the rate at which eviction
destroys a correct answer from $0.75$ to $0.026$ at a nominal $16\times$
budget, $3.4\times$ realized once the protected cache is counted, and raises
matched-cache accuracy by $3$-$19$pp above a crossover near one third of
retained cache. This is a batch-1, offline-scoring safeguard whose memory
saving decays toward $1\times$ by batch $16$ under static provisioning. A
geometry-based and an attention-free scorer collapse on the same inputs below
$25\%$ budget, indicating an information floor no scorer we tested removes and
recasting cache compression as per-input admission control rather than token
selection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Long-context inference spends most of its memory on the KV-cache, and eviction
is the standard remedy: score the prompt positions once, keep a fraction $b$,
and accept a small average accuracy cost. Recent work sharpens this trade
surprisingly. \citet{arxiv260509649} prove that selective retention
reduces a quantifiable \emph{attention dilution} term and report a learned-gate evictor exceeding the full cache on long-context dialogue,
\citet{arxiv260425975} derive a closed-form information-bottleneck score
producing the same paradox on LongBench, and an indexer-and-latent-memory
architecture~\citep{arxiv260525475} reports an information-density peak at
moderate compression. Their common claim is that less compute can yield more
accuracy.

Each result averages over a suite where the effect occurs, and a benchmark
mean positive in expectation can hide a subset of inputs on which eviction is
catastrophic. The two cases call for opposite actions. Where the answer is
spread over redundant tokens, discarding them concentrates attention and
eviction is safe. Where it sits in a handful of tokens, any scorer that drops
the wrong one loses it outright at every budget. Averaging the two populations
produces a number describing neither. Adaptive methods nonetheless train a
scorer~\citep{arxiv260509649, arxiv260525475, arxiv260203203, arxiv260210238}
or derive an unconditional capacity score from query
statistics~\citep{arxiv260425975}, and in both cases the decision is
\emph{which} tokens to keep. Whether the two populations are separable in the
pre-eviction attention alone, before any token is scored, is open. If they are,
the safe deployment of eviction is not a better scoring rule but a decision to
abstain.

We answer affirmatively. The \emph{head-agreement drop} on prefill attention,
the difference between mean Jaccard top-$k$ head agreement in early and late
layer-bins, partitions inputs into a dilution-prone class $\mathcal{D}$ and a
capacity-bound class $\mathcal{C}$, and a drop-in wrapper turns that partition
into a training-free policy. The signal reflects how heads specialize with depth. Heads agree broadly in
early layers and diverge in late ones, but when the answer occupies only a few
tokens those tokens hold them together and the divergence is shallow, so a
small drop is evidence that the cache holds little redundancy to discard.

Because a benchmark mean hides the failure mode we target, we evaluate \method
by what it prevents on the capacity-bound class rather than by a suite
average. It cuts the rate at which eviction destroys a correct answer by
$29\times$ at $16\times$ compression and lifts the accuracy-memory frontier at
matched \emph{achieved} cache above a crossover near $1/3$ kept cache, losing
below it. The converse deserves equal prominence: the gate is inert wherever
eviction is already safe, and in half the matrix cells of
Section~\ref{subsec:matrix} it opens on every non-capacity-bound input, so the
measured difference is exactly zero.

\paragraph{Contributions.}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An empirical partition.} Across eight (model, context) cells and
six RULER~\citep{arxiv240406654} task families (Section~\ref{sec:partition}),
three-key retrieval (NIAH-MK3) is the one task where eviction never recovers
accuracy, while five dilution-prone families recover wherever the model has
headroom. A distractor sweep confirms the mechanism, and the partition holds on
three LongBench subtasks the gate calls correctly a priori.

\item \textbf{A label-free predictor.} The early-to-late head-agreement drop
$D := \mathrm{agr}_{\text{early}} - \mathrm{agr}_{\text{late}}$ ranks the
capacity-bound task smallest and is the only one of six cheap prefill
statistics that separates the partition at all (Section~\ref{subsec:signal}).
It is one-sided: reliable at closing the gate on capacity-bound inputs, weakly
discriminative on dilution-prone ones, which bounds what the gate can promise.

\item \textbf{\method, a gating algorithm built on $D$.} One conditional on
the prefill attention, with no training and no labels
(Section~\ref{subsec:algo}). We set the threshold per model from an unlabeled
$z$-score pilot, which transfers across all four architecture families we test;
the fixed $\tau = 0.07$ we run everywhere is a within-family convenience that
fails on Qwen3 (Section~\ref{subsec:calibration}).

\item \textbf{Scorer-independence of the capacity limit.} A faithful
ManifoldKV+Ada-KV geometry scorer and the attention-free
KeyDiff~\citep{keydiff} both collapse on NIAH-MK3 below $25\%$ budget
(Section~\ref{subsec:scorer-indep}), even though the same scorer reproduces its
published two-key advantage. No scoring rule recovers information the retained
subset does not contain.

\item \textbf{Head-to-heads, and a scaling relation.} The gate lifts
matched-budget accuracy around CapKV and pretrained DBTrimKV
(Section~\ref{subsec:headtohead}), though on DBTrimKV the full-cache fallback
dominates and plain DBTrimKV wins at matched \emph{memory}. A closed form,
$\rho \approx (1 - A_{\mathrm{full}}) \cdot
p_{\mathrm{recov}}(\rho_{\mathcal{R}}^2/\gamma)$, unifies the scaling evidence
(Section~\ref{subsec:theory}), and a pre-registered 32K test preserves its
ordinal content while falsifying constant-ratio proportionality.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope.} We claim as novel neither the paradigm that less compute
can beat full attention nor the dilution mechanism, since
\citet{arxiv260509649} state both and we adopt their Proposition 3.1 and
Corollary 3.2, and we repurpose a known head-agreement primitive rather than
proposing a new statistic. We claim no compression win: at matched memory a
strong trained evictor beats us. Most of the headline $\Delta$ protects one
task family, since excluding NIAH-MK3 the grand mean falls from $+22.9$pp to
$+4.5$pp and a cheating task-label oracle reaches $+20.1$pp. The contribution
is the label-free per-input decision, not the compression ratio it delivers
where eviction was already safe.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

The nearest KV-eviction methods differ from ours in what they decide.
DBTrimKV~\citep{arxiv260509649}, whose dilution mechanism we adopt,
CapKV~\citep{arxiv260425975}, DynamicKV~\citep{arxiv241214838}, which also
shows task type modulates which KV positions matter, and
IndexMem~\citep{arxiv260525475} all choose \emph{which} tokens to keep under a
budget, as do the wrappers (VECTOR~\citealp{arxiv260523258},
CriticalKV~\citealp{arxiv250203805}) and the learned gates (Attention-Gate~\citealp{zeng2025incontextkvcacheevictionllms}, Fast
KVzip~\citealp{arxiv260117668}), which also require training. With the budget
fixed in advance, none represents the case where the correct action is to
spend no budget. EvicPress~\citep{arxiv251214946} does choose per context
\emph{whether} to evict, but as one term in a profiled quality-latency
optimization over storage tiers rather than a label-free call from prefill
attention. SAGE-KV~\citep{sagekv} comes closest, reading a one-shot post-prefill attention signal, but spends it choosing which tokens and heads to
keep and never identifies a class where eviction cannot be made safe.

Diagnostic work establishes that such a partition should exist without
providing a predictor. \citet{arxiv251000231} document that eviction degrades
some task types and \citet{arxiv260508234} ask when value-aware eviction
helps, but neither pairs a helping class with a hurting one on one axis or
predicts the side a priori. Our signal differs from the \emph{within-layer}
head consensus of \citet{arxiv260301426}, used there descriptively, in being a
directional \emph{cross-layer} contrast computed online and thresholded to
gate. Retrieval heads~\citep{wu2025retrieval} show a small, sparse head subset
drives long-context retrieval, the mechanism making capacity-bound inputs
fragile; $D$ measures that subset's layer-wise convergence rather than
identifying its members. We claim the conjunction absent from all of these: a
helps-versus-hurts partition on one axis, predicted a priori from a single
prefill scalar, deployed as a whether-to-evict gate, with the capacity-bound
side shown scorer-independent. We wrap SnapKV~\citep{arxiv240414469},
H2O~\citep{arxiv230614048}, StreamingLLM~\citep{arxiv230917453} and
PyramidKV~\citep{arxiv240602069}; Appendix~\ref{app:deferred} treats all
neighbors in full.

\section{The empirical partition}
\label{sec:partition}

A claim about inputs has to be established on inputs, so we demonstrate the
partition before proposing a predictor. This section asks whether a class
exists on which eviction never recovers accuracy, whether it survives outside
synthetic data, and whether simpler properties explain it away.

\paragraph{Setup.} We use RULER~\citep{arxiv240406654} via the
\texttt{simonjegou/ruler}~\citep{ruler-hf} port distributed with
kvpress~\citep{kvpress}, at $T \in \{4\mathrm{K}, 16\mathrm{K}\}$ on
Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B/14B-Instruct~\citep{arxiv241215115} and
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3~\citep{arxiv231006825}. Eviction is SnapKV-style by
default: score each prompt position by the average attention from the last $w
= 32$ queries across heads and layers, keep the top-$b$ positions for nine
budgets $b \in [0.0625, 1.0]$, and always preserve the first $n_{\text{sink}}
= 4$ sink positions~\citep{arxiv230917453} and last $w = 32$ recent positions.
Cache slicing uses explicit \texttt{position\_ids} to preserve RoPE
alignment~\citep{roformer} rather than the attention-mask trick that silently
shifts RoPE on evicted positions. Task codes are RULER's: VT, FWE,
QA\_1/QA\_2, and NIAH-MK$k$ = \texttt{niah\_multikey\_}$k$ ($k$ near-tie keys,
one carrying the answer). Full protocol in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

\paragraph{Metric.} A mean-accuracy comparison cannot express a claim about
individual inputs. For task $\mathsf{T}$, model $M$, context length $T$, and
$A(b, x)$ the accuracy on input $x$ at budget $b$, with $b_{\max} = 1$ the
full cache, we report the per-input recovery rate

\paragraph{The partition.} We measured $\rho$ on six RULER tasks across eight
(model, context) cells under attention-score eviction
(Table~\ref{tab:partition}, Appendix~\ref{app:deferred}). Five task families
clear $\rho \ge 0.05$ at some context length, while NIAH-MK3 stays at $\rho
\le 0.02$ on every model and every context. Per-cell counts are low, so the
claim rests on the pooled contrast, which is unambiguous: NIAH-MK3 recovers on
$2/526$ inputs ($\rho = 0.004$, Wilson $[0.001, 0.014]$) against, for example,
$65/680$ ($\rho = 0.096$, $[0.076, 0.120]$) for niah\_multivalue. Two effects explain the
cells that fall below threshold without contradicting the partition. Saturation
suppresses $\rho$ wherever $A_{\mathrm{full}} \to 1$ leaves no headroom, and the
same models recover at longer context, exactly as Eq.~\eqref{eq:scaling}
predicts. The remaining three exceptions are budget-insensitive rather than
saturated, in that headroom exists but the wrong inputs stay wrong at every
budget. All three are QA cells, pointing to parametric-knowledge failures that
no cache budget can fix. We claim the partition at 4K and 16K only, since a
single 32K probe puts NIAH-MK3 at $\rho = 0.04$, one point under the threshold
that defines the class (Section~\ref{subsec:prereg32k}).

\paragraph{The class has more than one member.} A single capacity-bound task
leaves open whether NIAH-MK3 names a class or an isolated construction, so we
tested the boundary on four further RULER tasks chosen by mechanism rather
than outcome: a second near-tie retrieval task predicted capacity-bound, a
real-hard-negative task predicted dilution-prone despite carrying the most
distractors in the suite, a distractor-free single-needle control predicted
dilution-prone, and a code-completion task needing ten exact low-redundancy
tokens predicted capacity-bound (Table~\ref{tab:capbound},
Appendix~\ref{app:deferred}).

The outcome rules out the two simplest alternative accounts. niah\_multiquery
is capacity-bound on two of three models and borderline on the third, giving
the class a confirmed second member. qa\_2, niah\_single\_1, and cwe are all
dilution-prone, which defeats both a distractor-count and an
exact-output-length account: qa\_2 carries the most distractors of any task
tested yet sits comfortably on the dilution-prone side, and cwe requires ten
exact low-redundancy tokens yet closes the gate on none of the $300$ inputs
across the three models. What separates the classes is whether the distractors
are near-tie surface-form variants of the answer, not their count or the
exact-token demand. The single-model exception, niah\_single\_1 opening on
$87\%$ of Qwen2.5-3B inputs against $0\%$ on the other two, tracks a scale
shift in $D$ rather than a mechanism failure: task ordering is preserved
across models (Spearman $\rho = 0.98$) but absolute scale compresses by
$2.4\times$ on the larger model, which the $z$-score calibration of
Section~\ref{subsec:calibration} corrects.

RULER is constructed, so a partition found only there would not license a
deployment claim. On three LongBench subtasks~\citep{arxiv230814508} with
Qwen2.5-14B the gate calls all three correctly \emph{a priori} from $D$ alone,
with no labels and no task identity (Table~\ref{tab:realistic-workload}). lcc
code completion~\citep{liu2024repobench} is genuinely capacity-bound: plain
SnapKV destroys $64\%$ of the answers the full cache gets right ($0.292 \to
0.135$ at $16\times$) while recovering almost none ($\rho = 0.031$), the
signature previously seen only on synthetic NIAH-MK3. Its mean drop $D =
0.054$ sits below $\tau$, the gate closes on $75\%$ of inputs, and accuracy
improves by $+0.146$. repobench-p is mixed and milder ($+0.049$), while multi-
hop hotpotqa~\citep{hotpotqa} is evict-robust, the gate opening on $86\%$ of
inputs and gaining nothing ($+0.000$): where eviction is safe the gate stays
out of the way. These subtasks are also our one estimate of how much capacity-
bound traffic a realistic workload carries.

\paragraph{Three controls.} A distractor sweep over the RULER NIAH-MultiKey family confirms the dilution
mechanism of \citet{arxiv260509649}, mean $D$ shrinking monotonically as near-tie distractors accumulate (Table~\ref{tab:distractor}). The partition is
unchanged under the heavier $10\%$ prefix-and-suffix protection of
\citet{arxiv260518053}, and uniform-random scoring destroys accuracy at every
budget, ruling out a generic regularization explanation
(Appendix~\ref{app:random}).

\section{The gating method: \method}
\label{sec:method}

Measured outcomes define the partition, but a deployed system cannot observe
them and needs a quantity computable before decoding. This section defines
that quantity, shows why it is the one to use, and states the algorithm it
drives.

\subsection{The head-agreement drop}
\label{subsec:signal}

Take the prefill attention tensor $[L, H, T, T]$ for $L$ layers, $H$ heads and
prompt length $T$. Project onto the last $w = 32$ rows, the observation
queries, and average for a per-(layer, head, key) attention vector. For each
layer and head pair take the Jaccard similarity of the two heads' top-$k$ key
sets ($k = 32$), averaged over pairs, giving per-layer agreement $a_\ell$.
Binning the $L$ layers into thirds, with $a_{\text{early}}$, $a_{\text{late}}$
the mean agreement in the first and last bins,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:D}
D := a_{\text{early}} - a_{\text{late}}.
\end{equation}
$D$ is one scalar per input costing $O(L H^2 k)$, or $17$ms on Qwen2.5-1.5B
and $89$ms on Mistral-7B at 4K. It is not free even when the base evictor
already scores by attention, since that scoring uses head-\emph{averaged}
attention whereas $D$ needs the per-head pattern, which requires materializing
attention that fused FlashAttention~\citep{flash-attention} does not produce.
This is the method's principal cost, treated in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

That cost is justified only if no cheaper statistic does the same job, so we
ran a same-inputs ablation against five alternatives from the same prefill
pass: mean attention entropy, top-$32$ attention mass share, max single-
position share, key-norm dispersion, and an early-minus-late entropy drop. $D$
is not merely the best but the only one separating the partition at all, its
NIAH-MK3 mean falling strictly below all four dilution-prone task means with
per-input AUC $1.000$ on the selecting cell, while every alternative fails
task-level separation and tops out at $0.805$. The dissection shows why: raw
attention entropy is at chance ($0.508$), its early-versus-late contrast
reaches $0.805$, and replacing entropy with head \emph{agreement} makes the
separation exact, so both the layer contrast and the agreement measure are
load-bearing (Table~\ref{tab:signal-ablation}). On Qwen2.5-1.5B at 4K the per-
task means separate cleanly, NIAH-MK3 at $D = 0.040$ below $\tau = 0.07$ while
the dilution-prone tasks run from FWE at $0.098$ to QA\_1 at $0.218$
(Figure~\ref{fig:phenomenon}).

An AUC of $1.000$ on the selecting cell is the number to trust least, so we
repeated the ablation on three cells that played no part in that selection.
$D$ remains the only one of the six separating tasks anywhere, on two of the
three held-out cells, but the number does not survive: held out, per-input AUC
is $0.899$, $0.978$, and $0.741$. The weak cell is Llama-3.1-8B, where
niah\_multivalue sits below NIAH-MK3, a limit Section~\ref{sec:limitations}
treats as real.

\subsection{Algorithm}
\label{subsec:algo}

With the signal validated, the policy is one conditional. Given a prompt $x$,
budget $b \in (0, 1]$, sink-and-recency protection $(n_{\text{sink}}, w)$
preserving the first $n_{\text{sink}}$ and last $w$ positions, and threshold
$\tau$,
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\
\method(x;\, \text{base}, b, \tau) =
\begin{cases}
\text{base.evict}(\text{cache}, b) & \text{if } D(\text{cache}.\text{attentions}) \ge \tau \\
\text{cache} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}\
}
\end{equation}
where prefill produces \texttt{cache} with its attentions, from which $D$
follows by Eq.~\eqref{eq:D}. The gate fires once per input at prefill, before
any decode step, with no backward pass, no training, and no per-token
decision. \method never observes correctness, making it label-free in an
accuracy sense.

The asymmetry between branches determines how the method should be read.
Closing the gate costs memory but not accuracy, since the full cache is what
the unevicted model would have used, while opening it delegates entirely to
the base evictor. \method inherits that behavior where it opens and adds a
floor where it closes, so the evaluation measures harm prevented rather than
accuracy gained.

\subsection{Calibrating the threshold}
\label{subsec:calibration}\label{sec:recipe}

The single free parameter is $\tau$, and how it is set determines whether the
method transfers. We recommend calibrating $\tau$ by standardizing $D$ per model over an
unlabeled $\sim 100$-input pilot from the serving distribution. This $z$-score
transformation transfers one global threshold zero-shot to every held-out
family we test, the Llama-architecture models (Yi-1.5-9B, Llama-3.1-8B) and
Qwen3-4B~\citep{Yi_tc,llama_tc,qwen_tc}. It needs no task labels and is the
only variant that transfers uniformly.

The fixed $\tau = 0.07$ behind every headline number is a within-family
convenience, not a generalizable constant. It mis-classifies Qwen2.5-3B at
16K, where layer-bin drops compress at long context, and fails outright on
Qwen3, whose drops sit at a different scale ($\mu = 0.034$, $\sigma = 0.017$)
placing $\tau = 0.07$ at $z = +2.0$ so the gate essentially never fires. We
report it uniformly so no cell is tuned, not because it generalizes. A sweep
on Qwen2.5-1.5B shows a plateau over $\tau \in [0.055, 0.10]$ within which
$\Delta$ varies by under $1$pp and false-positive and false-negative rates
stay at or below $9\%$, so the deployed value is not a knife-edge
(Figure~\ref{fig:tau-sensitivity}).

\paragraph{What the gate costs a deployment.} The gate-open rate
$p_{\text{open}}$ is a property of the workload rather than the method, so
realized compression is not a constant of \method and savings are bounded by
$1 - p_{\text{open}}$. Two consequences matter in deployment. Capacity
planning drifts as traffic shifts toward low-$D$ inputs, and because $D$ falls
as near-tie distractors accumulate, an adversary controlling prompt content
can force the fallback and turn a memory attack into a denial-of-capacity
attack. Batching compounds this, since a batch provisioned for its largest
resident cache realizes the \emph{minimum} compression over its sequences: at
$p_{\text{open}} = 0.75$ mean compression falls from $2.91\times$ at batch $1$
to $1.00\times$ by batch $16$. Both effects, their mitigations, and the per-model numbers are in Appendix~\ref{app:deferred}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

Models, contexts, eviction policies, and protection match
Section~\ref{sec:partition}. The matrix uses RULER 4K with the four-task mixed
suite ($N = 100$ each); the scaling table uses 4K and 16K. Gating deltas carry
$95\%$ cluster-bootstrap intervals, recovery rates Wilson intervals. We ask in
turn whether the gate prevents the harm it targets (\ref{subsec:harm}),
whether it depends on the base evictor (\ref{subsec:matrix}), whether it pays
for itself at matched memory (\ref{subsec:pareto}), and whether the capacity
limit belongs to the input or to our scorer (\ref{subsec:scorer-indep}).

\subsection{Does the gate prevent the failure it targets?}
\label{subsec:harm}

The motivating claim is about harm rather than average accuracy, so we measure
it directly: $\Pr_x[A(b, x) < A_{\mathrm{plain}}(1, x)]$ at fixed $b$, the
fraction of inputs on which a deployed system loses an answer the full cache
would have produced, for both arms at matched nominal budget over seven SnapKV
cells (Table~\ref{tab:harm-rate}, Appendix~\ref{app:deferred}).

The capacity-bound task carries the result. At $16\times$ compression plain
eviction destroys $75.0\%$ of the answers the full cache gets right on NIAH-MK3 while gating destroys $2.6\%$, a $29\times$ reduction holding across
budgets ($0.024$ against $0.724$ at $b = 0.25$). The other three tasks matter
equally. On QA\_1 the arms are numerically identical at every budget ($0.110$
against $0.110$ at $b = 0.0625$), the gate opening on every input so the
systems coincide. On VT at the most aggressive budget both are harmed at
nearly $70\%$ ($0.684$ against $0.694$), so the gate neither helps nor hurts
where the base evictor fails on inputs it correctly calls safe. Only FWE shows
partial protection ($0.236$ against $0.414$). The gate is not a general
accuracy improvement but a floor under one failure mode, inert elsewhere by
design.

The cleanest single cell makes the same point without aggregation. On Mistral
4K NIAH-MK3, plain SnapKV collapses from $0.99$ to $0.00$ at $b = 0.0625$
while gated SnapKV holds $0.89$ at every budget, closing on $90/100$ inputs
with $10/100$ false positives where it opens and eviction destroys the needle
(Figure~\ref{fig:mistral-showcase}).

\subsection{Does the result depend on the base evictor?}
\label{subsec:matrix}

A gate working with only one scoring rule would be a property of that rule. We
ran a $4 \times 4$ matrix of four base evictors (SnapKV, H2O, StreamingLLM,
PyramidKV) and four models under the same gate and $\tau$, with no per-method
tuning (Table~\ref{tab:matrix}, Appendix~\ref{app:deferred}). All sixteen
cells are positive, grand mean $+22.9$pp, every $95\%$ interval above zero
(smallest lower bound $+9.3$pp), establishing transfer across base evictors.

That grand mean is not the number to quote. Dropping NIAH-MK3 takes it to
$+4.5$pp, and in eight of sixteen cells the non-MK3 $\Delta$ is exactly
$0.000$, the gate opening on every non-MK3 input so the arms execute
identically. The matrix is close to one measurement, $\Delta_{\mathrm{MK3}} =
+0.780$, replicated sixteen times and diluted by the suite's one-quarter MK3
share. Read as a claim about a suite it misleads; read as a claim about
transfer it is what it should be, since a whether-to-evict gate ought to do
nothing when eviction is safe, and the zeros are an identity rather than a
null result. Per-task accuracy is additive under reweighting, so
for a workload with capacity-bound share $f$ the headline is $\Delta(f) = f
\Delta_{\mathrm{MK3}} + (1-f) \Delta_{\mathrm{rest}}$, near-linear through the
origin, with $\Delta(0) = +0.045$ and $\Delta(0.25) = +0.229$.

Three caveats bound the matrix. The plain arm uses a shared single keep-mask
and a clean per-head SnapKV collapses less sharply at $2\times$ ($0.32$ not
$0.14$ on MK3), so these deltas should be read at $\ge 4\times$, where the
collapse is scorer-independent; restricting the grid to $\{0.125, 0.25\}$
raises the grand mean by only $+1.7$pp. A clean per-head Ada-KV plain arm
costs $1.1$pp ($+25.8$ to $+24.7$pp at $b \le 0.25$), a wash rather than a
collapse, with one model-specific exception on Mistral-7B that a Llama-3.1-8B
control rules out as a grouped-query-geometry artifact
(Appendix~\ref{app:deferred}). The Qwen2.5-14B H2O column is separately not
independent, since that run used two-pass prefill under which H2O's all-query
score reduces exactly to SnapKV's, leaving fifteen independent cells.

Beyond the matrix, SnapKV runs on Yi-1.5-9B ($\Delta = +39.0$pp) and
Llama-3.1-8B ($+10.9$pp) extend the mechanism to two more architectures, and a
32B-scale run confirms the predictor scales while illustrating the saturation
arm, that model being near-saturated at 4K so the gate's value is protection
rather than recovery. Comparing best plain against best gated evictor per
model checks the strongest baseline
(Tables~\ref{tab:crossarch},~\ref{tab:bestplain}). Composition also extends
outside the attention-score family, lifting a fifth, geometry-based
(ManifoldKV-style) base evictor by $\Delta = +16.2$pp. That variant uses a
single shared mask forced by our uniform-cache harness and does not reproduce
ManifoldKV's reported multi-key rescue, so it tests composition rather than
standing in for the faithful implementation of
Section~\ref{subsec:scorer-indep}.

\subsection{Does the gate pay for itself at matched memory?}
\label{subsec:pareto}

A closed gate holds the full cache, so matched-\emph{nominal}-budget
comparison flatters the gated arm; the honest comparison is at matched
\emph{achieved} kept-KV (Figure~\ref{fig:pareto-gated},
Appendix~\ref{app:deferred}). Gating beats plain SnapKV by $3$ to $19$pp once
the target retains more than about a third of the cache, and loses below that,
where the full-KV fallback floors compression near $3\times$. \method is a moderate-compression safeguard rather than an aggressive-regime
compressor, recovering $69\%$ of the headroom available to a per-input oracle.
What a deployment compresses is set by the gate-open fraction rather than the
nominal budget, since $1 - p_{\text{open}}$ of the cache stays resident
whatever $b$ is.

Whether per-input resolution earns its cost is testable against a policy
knowing each input's task label. Against such an oracle \method reaches
$+22.9$pp against $+20.1$pp, matching it exactly in eight of sixteen cells, so
most of the matrix $\Delta$ is a task-level effect a cheating baseline could
reproduce. The per-input claim rests on the cells where \method exceeds it,
most clearly Llama-3.1-8B NIAH-MK3, where the task mean $D = 0.071$ sits
\emph{above} $\tau$: any policy keyed on that mean opens everywhere and gains
nothing, while per-input dispersion closes the gate on $\approx 44\%$ of
inputs and recovers $+43.5$pp.

This matches how $D$ orders tasks: it ranks NIAH-MK3 smallest in every Qwen
and Mistral cell, and on the 4K/16K cells $\rho / (1 - A_{\mathrm{full}})$
fits a through-origin slope of $0.34$ on dilution-prone tasks while staying
$\le 0.02$ on NIAH-MK3 regardless of headroom.

\subsection{Is the capacity limit a property of the input or of our scorer?}
\label{subsec:scorer-indep}

A better scoring rule might make the capacity-bound class evictable and render
the gate unnecessary. The framing depends on ruling this out, so we tested two
scorers from outside the attention-score family: a faithful
ManifoldKV~\citep{arxiv260208343} implementation scoring $s_i = \|k_i -
\mu\|_2$ against the per-(layer, kv-head) key centroid, paired with Ada-KV~\citep{arxiv240711550} per-head budget reallocation, and the attention-free
KeyDiff~\citep{keydiff}.

A negative result is interpretable only if the scorer is competent. On two-key
NIAH the geometry scorer reproduces the multi-key advantage its authors
report, reaching $0.72$ at $b = 0.5$ where SnapKV is at $0.16$. On three-key
NIAH-MK3 the same scorer, in all four allocation variants, sits at or near
$0.00$ against a full-cache $0.66$ once the budget falls below $25\%$, and
KeyDiff does the same.

The gate is built on the conclusion this licenses. On capacity-bound inputs
the recoverable information is not present in any $25\%$ subset of the cache,
so no scoring rule, attention-based, geometric, or attention-free, recovers
it, and the only safe action is not to evict. The result holds at $\ge
4\times$ compression rather than $2\times$, consistent with the caveat on
Table~\ref{tab:matrix}.

\subsection{Head-to-heads with published methods}
\label{subsec:headtohead}\label{subsec:capkv-longbench}\label{subsec:dbtrimkv}

We also wrapped published evictors the gate was not designed around. Around a
CapKV re-implementation~\citep{arxiv260425975} on LongBench it is weakly
positive ($+1.5$/$+8.3$pp, $N = 68$ and $24$), and around pretrained
DBTrimKV~\citep{arxiv260509649} it lifts matched-budget accuracy by $+23.3$pp
on RULER 4K.

That second number is a memory-spending win rather than a matched-memory one.
At $\tau = 0.07$ the gate fires on only $1.7\%$ of DBTrimKV records, so the
wrapped system holds $\approx 14.5\times$ the cache of the plain one, and at
matched \emph{memory} plain DBTrimKV \emph{beats} it. The rare firing is the
Qwen3 scale mismatch of Section~\ref{subsec:calibration} in a head-to-head,
since DBTrimKV's pretrained gates exist only for Qwen3 variants; the per-model
$z$-scored threshold ($\tau_{\mathrm{Qwen3}} = 0.022$) restores a genuinely
compressing gate at $43\%$ kept-KV with $\Delta = +0.235$. \method does not
advance the frontier over a strong trained method at matched memory. Its value
is the label-free decision to keep the full cache when eviction is unsafe.

\subsection{Mechanism and scaling}
\label{subsec:theory}\label{sec:theory}

We adopt the dilution mechanism of \citet{arxiv260509649} verbatim. For a head
reading a relevant set $\mathcal{R}$ against noise $\mathcal{N}$ with
pre-eviction signal mass $\alpha_{\mathcal{R}}$, near-tie distractors force
the dilution $\delta_t$ large and preferential retention reduces it, so the
partition follows: capacity-bound tasks concentrate the signal
($\alpha_{\mathcal{R}} \to 1$), dilution-prone tasks spread it
($\alpha_{\mathcal{R}} \ll 1$). With the accuracy headroom $1 -
A_{\mathrm{full}}(M, T)$ this gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:scaling}
\rho_{\mathrm{KV}}(\mathsf{T}, M, T) \;\approx\;
\bigl(1 - A_{\mathrm{full}}(M, T)\bigr) \cdot p_{\mathrm{recov}}\bigl(\rho_{\mathcal{R}}^2 / \gamma\bigr),
\qquad p_{\mathrm{recov}}: [0, \infty) \to [0, 1],
\end{equation}
with $\rho_{\mathcal{R}}$ the retained fraction of relevant mass and $\gamma$
the noise-retention ratio. We test only the headroom factor, holding
$p_{\mathrm{recov}}$ fixed, so the recovery factor is a structural
placeholder, and the surrogate motivates the predictor on the dilution-prone
side only: on the capacity-bound side the bridge from $D$ to $\delta_t$ needs
$\kappa \ge 10.11$ while the measured NIAH-MK3 value is $\kappa = 3.33$. Our
claims therefore rest on the observed partition rather than the bridge (Append
ices~\ref{app:proof-scaling},~\ref{app:sufficient},~\ref{app:sharpened}).

\paragraph{A pre-registered out-of-range test at 32K.}
\label{subsec:prereg32k}
Every scaling result above is in-range, so we pre-registered an extrapolation
test: fit $\rho / (1 - A_{\mathrm{full}})$ on 4K/16K, measure
$A_{\mathrm{full}}$ at 32K (Qwen2.5-1.5B, $N = 100$), and hash the predicted
intervals before sweeping.

One of three predictions lands strictly inside its interval. VT exceeding its
band is conservative, while NIAH-MK3 rising to $0.04$ against a $\rho \le
0.02$ pin is an anti-conservative miss whose Wilson interval still covers
$0.02$. A second draw replicates FWE and NIAH-MK3 but not VT, so we drop the
claim that eviction beats full-KV on VT in mean accuracy. What survives is
ordinal, MK3's ratio staying far below VT's at any length even as constant-ratio proportionality fails. We claim a directional relation, not a law, and
do not extend the partition beyond 16K.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Four limits bound the gate's value, stated here rather than left implicit;
Appendix~\ref{app:deferred} adds seven narrower ones.

\emph{The signal is not free.} It needs materialized prefill attention, which
fused FlashAttention and paged-attention serving~\citep{kwon2023efficient} do
not provide. Our two-pass mode keeps the extra pass to $O(w \cdot T)$ rather
than $O(T^2)$, but it remains a pass a vanilla stack does not run, at
$O(LH^2k)$ cost growing in head count. \method therefore suits deployments
that can afford one such pass or already run one for attention-scored
eviction. KeyDiff presses this hardest, being attention-free for exactly this
reason, but it too falls to chance on NIAH-MK3 below $25\%$ budget
(Section~\ref{subsec:scorer-indep}), so its cheapness does not buy safety on
the class the signal guards.

\emph{The safety property rests on a surrogate.} On a single-head
deterministic-SNR surrogate we obtain a two-sided scaling bound, a converse
vacuous at our $(H, L, T)$, and an approximate useful-set identification that
fails on the capacity-bound side. These are properties of the surrogate, not
the deployed system, so our claims rest on the empirical partition.

\emph{The threshold is only partly portable.} On Yi-1.5-9B and Llama-3.1-8B
the drop ordering transfers but $\tau = 0.07$ does not, mis-classifying
niah\_multivalue on both and inverting the drop-versus-$\rho$ prediction on
Yi. The $z$-scored variant repairs this from an unlabeled pilot but remains a
calibration step, and per-input separation stays at AUC $\approx 0.80$ there,
a gap traceable to the non-monotone depth profile on Llama.

\emph{The gate is a detector, not a calibrated predictor.} It is one-sided: on
LongBench passage\_count it closes on all inputs (mean $D = -0.013$) although
eviction is safe there, a benign false positive costing compression rather
than accuracy. The scorer-independence result likewise holds at $\ge 4\times$,
not $2\times$, and multilingual tasks remain untested.

\section{Conclusion}

KV-cache eviction is deployed as if its cost were uniform across inputs, and
it is not. Where the answer is spread over redundant tokens compression is
safe; where it sits in a few near-tie tokens eviction destroys three quarters
of the answers the full cache gets right. One label-free prefill statistic, the
early-to-late head-agreement drop, predicts which side an input falls on
before any token is generated. \method turns that into a collapse-avoiding safeguard: the harm rate at
$16\times$ compression falls from $0.750$ to $0.026$ and a $99\% \to 0\%$
collapse holds flat on Mistral-7B NIAH-MK3, across four base evictors, four
model families, and \texttt{lcc} code completion. It costs one prefill pass
that exposes attention, and loses at matched memory to a strong trained
evictor.

\subsection*{Reproducibility statement}

Our SnapKV-family runs (the partition, the matrix, the scaling and 32K sweeps,
and the LongBench probes) use single NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs with PyTorch 2.11,
Transformers 5.9, and Python 3.13, in bfloat16 with greedy (argmax) decoding,
\texttt{max\_new\_tokens = 128}, and a fixed seed, with three input-draw
replicates on five SnapKV cells (Appendix~\ref{app:ci}). The two published
head-to-heads run in the baselines' own pinned environments: the DBTrimKV
comparison uses the authors' \texttt{trimkv} environment (Transformers 4.57.1,
PyTorch 2.8, FlashAttention 2), and the CapKV comparison is our
re-implementation in an isolated environment, both documented with the released
logs. Models are the public Hugging Face checkpoints of Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B/14B/32B-Instruct,
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Yi-1.5-9B-Chat, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, the last two of which carry the cross-architecture and
DBTrimKV results respectively. Tasks come from the
\texttt{simonjegou/ruler} port of RULER~\citep{ruler-hf,kvpress} and from
LongBench~\citep{arxiv230814508}. Cache slicing preserves RoPE alignment via
explicit \texttt{position\_ids}, and 16K runs use SDPA prefill with an eager
re-forward of the $w = 32$ observation window for scoring
(Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). Each table caption states its per-cell sample
sizes. The gate is one thresholded scalar (Eq.~\eqref{eq:D}), so anyone can
recompute every gated number in the paper from the released per-input logs.
Code, sweep configurations, and raw per-input jsonl outputs will be released
with the camera-ready.

\subsection*{Ethics statement}

This work studies inference-time efficiency of publicly released language
models on public benchmarks. It involves no human subjects, no personal data,
and no new model training. Better KV-cache eviction lowers the memory and
energy cost of long-context inference, and we see no ethical risk specific to
this method beyond those of the underlying models.
